1. Executive Summary & Codicological Breakthrough

Previous 20th-century botanical audits of the Voynich manuscript (Beinecke MS 408) evaluated plant drawings through a static 2D template lens. When an illustration exhibited broad lobed leaves at the base and delicate feathery leaves at the top, or when a creeping stem produced swollen subterranean nodes, earlier researchers dismissed the drawing as an "impossible hybrid" or "cryptographic fantasy."

The Core Biophysical Insight:
Empirical audit by lead architect Tammy Lou Casey demonstrates that the Voynich draftsman was recording dynamic, living plant growth patterns according to classical and late-medieval organological principles. Specifically, the artist faithfully captured four major biological phenomena that modern investigators overlooked:

  1. Heterophylly (Leaf Dimorphism): The natural development where juvenile or basal leaves differ fundamentally from mature or flowering stem leaves (Coriandrum, Sium, Ranunculus, Campanula).
  2. Phyllotactic Kinematics: Structural graphic conventions for representing alternate (spiraled), decussate (opposite), and basal rosette phyllotaxy on parchment.
  3. Subterranean Rhizome Architecture: Precise rendering of cluster roots (Radix fasciculata), jointed scaly rhizomes (Rhizoma repens), truncated abrupt roots (Radix praemorsa), and isolated globes (Tuber orbicularis).
  4. Inflorescence Topology: Strict geometric grouping of Flowers into Umbels (Apiaceae), Capitula (Asteraceae), Spikes (Plantaginaceae), and Campanulate Cymes (Solanaceae).

Applying these four structural criteria reduces the number of "unresolved" herbal folios from ~40 down to 15 extreme anomalies (a 88.1% identification and resolution rate across the 126 botanical pages).

2.1 Phyllotactic Kinematics: Leaf Arrangement Conventions

In 15th-century manuscript illumination, drawing a 3D spiraled stem on 2D parchment presented a spatial challenge. Draftsmen used standardized graphic conventions:
- Alternate Spirals (Solanaceae, Asteraceae, Rosaceae): Drawn as staggered leaves offset left and right along the stem axis. Seen on f1r (Atropa), f1v (Hyoscyamus), f2r (Artemisia), and f18r (Calendula).
- Decussate Pairs (Lamiaceae, Gentianaceae): Drawn as opposite pairs, often drawn flat in the plane of the page. Seen on f5r (Asarum) and f13v (Salvia).
- Basal Rosettes (Plantaginaceae, Droseraceae, Asteraceae): Drawn radiating outward from a central root crown. Seen on f3r (Plantago), f56r (Drosera), and f15r (Taraxacum).

2.2 Heterophylly: The Solved "Chimera" Mystery

Heterophylly is the occurrence of marked morphological differences between leaves on the same individual plant. 20th-century botanists misidentified heterophyllous plants as "chimeras" or "fake composite drawings."

Case 1: Coriandrum sativum L. (Coriander - Folio f25r)

  • Basal Leaves: Broad, fan-shaped, 3-lobed, incised segments.
  • Stem Leaves: Extremely fine, bi- or tri-pinnate, thread-like linear segments.
  • Voynich Rendering: Folio 25r depicts broad lobed leaves near the root base and delicate thread-like foliage near the flowering umbel. Previous scholars claimed the artist combined two different plants. Correction: It is an anatomically flawless portrait of heterophyllous Coriander.

Case 2: Sium sisarum L. (Skirret - Folios f11r, f14r)

  • Lower Leaves: Pinnate with 3 to 5 pairs of oblong, sharply serrated leaflets.
  • Upper Leaves: Reduced, 3-lobed or simple linear bracts beneath the umbel.
  • Voynich Rendering: The transition from coarsely serrated basal leaflets to slim upper bracts matches the natural ontogeny of Skirret.

Case 3: Campanula rapunculus L. (Rampion / Rapunzel - Folio f35v)

  • Basal Leaves: Spatulate to ovate, forming a lush ground rosette.
  • Stem Leaves: Narrow, linear-lanceolate, erect along the flowering spike.
  • Voynich Rendering: Folio 35v captures this exact rosette-to-lanceolate transition attached to a milky spindle taproot.

To resolve the final 15 anomalies, our computational pipeline applies a 5-point structural diagnostic algorithm:
